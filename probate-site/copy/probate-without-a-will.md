# Probate without a will

When someone dies without a valid will, they have died "intestate". The estate does not go to whoever the family assumes. The law sets out who inherits and who is allowed to deal with the estate. This page explains the general position in England and Wales. It is not legal advice, and your own circumstances may change the answer.

## Is it still called probate?

People use the word loosely, but strictly the court document is different. When there is a will with an executor, the court issues a grant of probate. When there is no will, or no executor able to act, the court issues letters of administration. The person who applies is called the administrator rather than the executor. The job is much the same.

## Who inherits?

The rules apply in a fixed order. Roughly:

1. A husband, wife or civil partner and children. How the estate is split depends on its size and who survives.
2. If there is no spouse or civil partner, the children share the estate.
3. If there are no children, then parents, then brothers and sisters, then half-brothers and half-sisters, then grandparents, then aunts and uncles, then half-aunts and half-uncles.
4. If no relative can be found, the estate passes to the Crown.

Some points that surprise people:

- **Unmarried partners do not inherit automatically,** however long the couple lived together. They may be able to make a claim to the court, but that is not the same as inheriting.
- **Step-children** do not inherit under the intestacy rules.
- **Friends and carers** do not inherit under these rules.
- **A divorced spouse** is generally treated as no longer a spouse once the divorce is final.
- **Assets that pass outside the estate,** such as a jointly owned home that passes to the survivor, are not affected by the intestacy rules.

## Who can apply to deal with the estate?

Those with the right to apply are, broadly, in the same order as those who inherit: the spouse or civil partner first, then children, and so on. Someone who is entitled can choose not to apply. If nobody with a stronger claim acts, others may be able to apply after them. A solicitor can tell you where you stand.

## What happens next

1. **Look for a will first.** Check with the bank, solicitors the person used, and any will-storage service. Wills can turn up late.
2. **Register the death** and get several copies of the certificate.
3. **List what the person owned and owed,** and who the relatives are.
4. **Decide who will apply** as administrator.
5. **Check if a grant is needed.** See [do I need probate?](/do-i-need-probate/)
6. **Apply for letters of administration,** and deal with any inheritance tax.
7. **Collect the assets, pay the debts and distribute** according to the intestacy rules.

## Why a will matters

Without a will, the estate may go to people the person would not have chosen, and families can end up in disputes. It can also take longer. See [how long probate takes](/how-long-does-probate-take/) and [how much probate costs](/how-much-does-probate-cost/).

If you think someone is not properly provided for, see [contesting a will](/contesting-a-will/). The same time limits and rules about claims can apply when there is no will.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I get probate without a will?

You apply for letters of administration instead of probate. The court needs to be satisfied that you are entitled to apply.

### Who inherits if there is no will and no family?

If no relative can be found, the estate passes to the Crown. This is rare, and a search for relatives usually comes first.

### Does my partner inherit if we were not married?

Not automatically. An unmarried partner does not inherit under the intestacy rules. They may be able to apply to the court for provision in some circumstances, and time limits apply.

### Does the spouse get everything?

Not always. It depends on the size of the estate and whether there are children. A solicitor can explain the split for your case.

### How long does it take?

Often longer than with a will, as there is more paperwork. See [how long does probate take](/how-long-does-probate-take/).

### Can I do it myself?

Yes, in many cases, through gov.uk. A solicitor can help where relationships are complicated, the estate is large, or people disagree.

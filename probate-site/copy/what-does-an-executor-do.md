# What does an executor do?

An executor is the person named in a will to deal with the estate of someone who has died. In practice, that means finding out what the person owned and owed, paying what is due, and passing the rest to the people entitled to it. This page explains the general position in England and Wales and is not legal advice.

## The main jobs

- **Arrange the funeral,** unless someone else has. Reasonable funeral costs are normally paid from the estate.
- **Register the death** and get copies of the death certificate. If someone else registered it, you will still need copies.
- **Find the will and the assets.** Make a list of bank accounts, property, pensions, insurance, investments and valuables, and of debts, loans and bills.
- **Value the estate** as at the date of death.
- **Deal with inheritance tax.** Many estates do not owe any, but HMRC still needs to be told the value in most cases.
- **Apply for the grant of probate** if one is needed. See [do I need probate?](/do-i-need-probate/)
- **Collect the assets,** close accounts and sell property if the will or circumstances require it.
- **Pay debts and tax,** and keep records.
- **Pay the beneficiaries** as the will directs, and keep proper accounts.
- **Keep everything safe** until it is distributed. That includes insuring and securing an empty property.

## Who can be an executor?

Any adult who is willing, including a beneficiary. It is common for a spouse or a child to act. A will can name one executor or several. Up to four can be named on a grant. <!-- TODO: confirm the limit of four on gov.uk or with a solicitor. -->

## Can I say no?

Yes. Being named does not force you to act. You can step back, but you should do it formally and before you start dealing with the estate, because acting can be treated as accepting the role. ## Are executors paid?

Not usually. Executors are entitled to reasonable expenses out of the estate, but unpaid work is the norm for family executors. Some wills allow a professional executor to charge. See [how much probate costs](/how-much-does-probate-cost/) if you are thinking of using a solicitor.

## How long does it take?

It varies a lot. See [how long does probate take](/how-long-does-probate-take/). Plan for months rather than weeks, and for longer if there is a property to sell.

## What are the risks?

An executor can be personally responsible for mistakes. Examples:

- Paying out to beneficiaries before debts and tax are known
- Missing a tax deadline, which can lead to interest or penalties
- Not insuring an empty property
- Not keeping records
- Ignoring a creditor you could have found

## When to get help

You do not have to use a solicitor, and many executors manage a simple estate themselves. Help is worth considering if:

- there is no will, or the will is unclear
- there is inheritance tax to pay
- a property needs to be sold or transferred
- there are overseas assets or a business
- beneficiaries disagree
- you are unsure what you are liable for

## Frequently asked questions

### Can an executor be a beneficiary?

Yes. It is very common.

### Can there be more than one executor?

Yes. A will can name several. They normally act together.

### What if an executor does not do their job?

Beneficiaries can ask for an update first. If that fails, a solicitor can explain whether the court can be asked to step in. See [how long does probate take](/how-long-does-probate-take/) for what is normal.

### Can I be sued as an executor?

If you make a mistake that causes loss, you could be personally responsible. This is one reason to keep records and take advice if you are not sure.

### What if there is no executor?

If nobody named can act, someone else can apply for letters of administration. See [probate without a will](/probate-without-a-will/).

### Do I need to open a bank account for the estate?

Many executors do, because it keeps the estate's money separate and gives a clear record. It is not always required.

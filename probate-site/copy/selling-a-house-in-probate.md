# Selling a house in probate

Selling a house is often the longest and most expensive part of dealing with an estate. This page explains the general position in England and Wales. It is not legal or tax advice.

## Can you sell before you have the grant?

In most cases you can start the process, but you cannot complete the sale until the executor or administrator has legal authority to sell. That authority normally comes from the grant of probate or letters of administration, if the property was in the deceased's sole name. If the house was owned jointly and passes to the survivor, the position can be different. See [do I need probate?](/do-i-need-probate/)

Estate agents and buyers often understand that probate takes time. Many will wait. Check with the agent, and take advice before you commit to a date.

## Who decides whether to sell?

The will usually sets this out. Executors have to follow it. If the will is silent, the executors normally decide, and beneficiaries have a say if they own the house between them. Disagreements can hold things up. A solicitor can help.

## Steps in selling

1. **Secure and insure the property.** An empty house may not be covered by standard home insurance. Tell the insurer.
2. **Get a valuation.** A date-of-death valuation is usually needed for inheritance tax and the probate application. See [how much probate costs](/how-much-does-probate-cost/).
3. **Apply for the grant** if one is needed.
4. **Instruct an estate agent and a conveyancer.** The executor, not the beneficiaries, normally signs.
5. **Sell, and pay off any mortgage,** then add the net proceeds to the estate.
6. **Pay debts and tax,** then distribute what is left.

## Costs to expect

- Estate agent fee
- Conveyancing fees
- Energy performance certificate and other searches
- Insurance and utility bills while the house is empty
- Council tax or exemptions, depending on the situation
- Any repairs or clearance you decide to do before sale

## Tax to ask about

If the estate is worth enough, inheritance tax may be due, and property is often the biggest part. If the house is sold for more than its value at death, capital gains tax can arise on the increase. ## What can slow a sale down

- Waiting for the grant
- A will that is unclear, or a dispute between beneficiaries
- Beneficiaries who want to keep the house or buy it
- A mortgage or equity release to be settled
- Problems with the title, or an unregistered property
- Someone still living there

## Can a beneficiary keep the house?

Sometimes. If the will leaves it to them, it can be transferred rather than sold. If it is shared, the beneficiaries may agree that one buys the others out. This needs legal paperwork and, often, a valuation.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I put the house on the market before probate?

Usually yes, but completion generally has to wait for the grant. Talk to the agent about how they will handle that.

### Who pays the estate agent?

The estate pays, out of the sale proceeds, where the sale is made by the executor.

### Do I need a solicitor to sell a house in probate?

You need a conveyancer to do the legal side of the sale. You may also want a probate solicitor to deal with the grant and the estate. They can be the same firm.

### How long does it take?

It varies a lot. See [how long does probate take](/how-long-does-probate-take/). The sale itself often takes several months once the grant is in place.

### What if the house is worth less than expected?

The estate's value depends on the sale price, and a lower figure can affect what beneficiaries receive. Ask for advice if prices have moved a lot since the date-of-death valuation.

### What if the house has a mortgage?

The lender is normally repaid from the sale proceeds. Tell the lender about the death early.

**Title tag:** How Long Does Probate Take in the UK? Realistic Timescales

**Meta description:** Getting the grant often takes weeks, but settling an estate usually takes six to twelve months or more. Here is what affects the timing and how to avoid delays.

# How long does probate take?

There are two different timescales, and people often mix them up.

1. **Getting the grant of probate.** This is the court document that gives the executor legal authority to deal with the estate. Once the application is in, this stage commonly takes a few weeks, sometimes longer.
2. **Finishing the whole estate.** This means collecting the money, paying the debts and tax, selling or transferring property and handing out what is left. For most estates this takes somewhere between six months and a year. Estates with a property to sell, a tax bill or a family dispute can take well over a year.

Nobody can give you an exact date at the start. What follows should help you work out which parts of the process apply to you.

## The short answer

- Before you can apply, you need to value the estate and, in many cases, deal with inheritance tax paperwork. This stage alone often takes a few weeks to a few months.
- The court then reviews the application and issues the grant. Recent figures put the average at around five weeks from application to grant, with complete online applications generally quicker than paper ones. Applications that are stopped because information is missing can take many months longer. Times change month to month, so check the current figures on gov.uk.
- After the grant, most executors need at least several months to finish. A common rule of thumb among solicitors is around six to twelve months from the date of death for a straightforward estate.

## The stages, and how long each tends to take

### 1. Registering the death and finding the will

You need the death certificate before anything else. Registration is normally done within five days of the death in England and Wales (eight days in Scotland). Then you need to find the original will, if there is one. Hunting for a missing will can add weeks.

### 2. Valuing the estate

You need to find out what the person owned and owed on the date they died: bank accounts, pensions, property, investments, debts and so on. Banks and other institutions usually need the death certificate and may take a couple of weeks each to reply with balances. Property usually needs a professional valuation.

### 3. Inheritance tax and the application forms

Most estates do not pay inheritance tax, but you still have to report the value to HMRC in some form. Where a full report is needed, it can take time to pull together. If tax is due, it must be paid, or arrangements made for paying it, before the court will normally issue the grant. For estates with a property that cannot yet be sold, there are ways to pay in instalments.

### 4. Applying for the grant

You can apply online or on paper. The application needs the original will (if there is one), the death certificate and the inheritance tax reference. Online applications are generally dealt with faster than paper ones, but both can be held up if something is missing or needs checking.

### 5. After the grant: collecting, paying, distributing

With the grant, you can close accounts, sell property, pay debts and then pay out the beneficiaries. Selling a house on the open market is often the longest single step. Final accounts are normally prepared last, and beneficiaries are only paid in full once the executor is sure all debts and tax are settled.

## What makes probate take longer

- **A property to sell.** Marketing, offers, conveyancing and chains can add several months.
- **No will.** When there is no valid will, someone has to apply for the right to deal with the estate and the court has rules on who can do that. This usually adds time and paperwork.
- **A home-made will that is unclear or looks unusual.** The court may ask questions about how the will was signed or witnessed.
- **Inheritance tax.** Extra forms, valuations and conversations with HMRC.
- **Overseas assets.** Different countries have different rules and timelines.
- **Family disagreements.** A dispute about the will, the value of items or who should act can stop everything until it is resolved.
- **Missing paperwork.** Lost account details, unknown pension providers or unclear ownership of assets.
- **Executors who live far apart,** or who disagree, or who are elderly or unwell.
- **A caveat.** Someone can lodge a caveat to stop a grant being issued. This lasts for a limited period and can be renewed.

## Are there deadlines I need to know about?

There is no fixed deadline for applying for probate, but some dates matter.

- **Inheritance tax.** Where tax is due, it is normally payable by the end of the sixth month after the month of death. Interest can be charged after that date. <!-- TODO: confirm the six-month rule and interest wording against gov.uk inheritance tax guidance. -->

## Can probate be sped up?

Sometimes, within limits. Things that usually help:

- Gather the death certificate, the original will, account numbers, property deeds and pension details early.
- Ask the banks to value the accounts at date of death as soon as you have the death certificate.
- Value the property promptly.
- Apply online where you can.
- Answer any query from the court quickly.
- Be realistic about the property sale. If you are able to, instruct the estate agent once you have authority to do so.

## Does probate take longer in Scotland or Northern Ireland?

The process and the name are different. In Scotland the equivalent is called confirmation, and in Northern Ireland the application is made through the Probate Office in Belfast. The timescales and fees are not the same as in England and Wales, so this page does not cover them. Speak to a solicitor in the relevant country.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does probate take if there is no will?

It usually takes longer. Someone has to apply as an administrator, following the order of priority set by law (generally a spouse or civil partner, then children, and so on), and extra paperwork is needed. Exact times vary, so ask a solicitor about your case.

### How long does probate take for a small estate?

If the estate is small and there is no property, a grant may not be needed at all, because banks often release small balances without one. Each bank sets its own limit. If a grant is not needed, things can move much faster. Our guide to [whether you need probate](/do-i-need-probate/) explains more.

### Can I sell the house before probate is granted?

You can usually market the house, but in most cases you cannot complete the sale until you have the grant, because the executor needs legal authority to sell. A solicitor can explain how this works in practice.

### Can I access the money in the bank before probate?

Banks often allow payment of funeral bills from the deceased's account with the invoice and the death certificate. Other withdrawals normally need the grant. Policies differ between banks.

### Do I have to pay inheritance tax before I get probate?

Where inheritance tax is due on the estate, it generally has to be dealt with before the grant is issued, though in some cases it can be paid in instalments. Many estates owe nothing.

### Is there a time limit to apply for probate?

There is no fixed deadline for applying, but delay can create problems, including interest on unpaid inheritance tax and trouble selling or insuring a property.

### What happens if the executor takes too long?

If an executor is not acting, beneficiaries can write to them asking for an update first. If that fails, a solicitor can advise on whether to ask the court to take action. Those steps can be expensive, so they are normally a last resort.

# Contesting a will

If you think a will is invalid, or that you have been left out unfairly, you may have options. Disputes over wills are stressful, can be expensive and often damage relationships, so it helps to understand the basics before you speak to anyone.

This page explains the general position in England and Wales. It is not legal advice, and the outcome of any case depends on its own facts.

## Two different kinds of challenge

People talk about "contesting a will" to mean two different things.

1. **Saying the will is not valid.** The argument is that the will should not count, for example because it was not signed correctly or the person did not understand what they were signing.
2. **Saying the will, or the lack of one, does not provide for you properly.** The will itself may be valid, but the argument is that you should receive something (or more) from the estate.

The grounds, the evidence and the time limits differ, so it is worth knowing which one you are thinking about.

## Common grounds for saying a will is invalid

- **It was not signed and witnessed correctly.** The law has formal requirements for how wills are made.
- **The person lacked mental capacity** when they made it. This is about whether they understood what they were doing, what they owned and who might expect to inherit.
- **They did not know and approve of the contents.** For example, if the will was read out wrongly or they could not read it.
- **Undue influence.** Someone pressured them so that the will reflects the other person's wishes rather than their own. Pressure alone is not enough: it has to amount to coercion, which is a high bar.
- **Fraud or forgery.** The will is fake, or was made by deception.
- **Mistakes.** In limited circumstances, a clerical error or misunderstanding can be put right.

Suspicion is not enough. A court will want evidence, such as medical records, the solicitor's file note and the testimony of witnesses.

## Claims for reasonable financial provision

Under the Inheritance (Provision for Family and Dependants) Act 1975, certain people can ask the court to order that more is provided for them from the estate, even if the will is valid. This can include:

- A spouse or civil partner
- A former spouse or civil partner who has not remarried
- A child of the deceased, including an adult child
- Someone treated as a child of the family
- A person living with the deceased as a partner, in some cases
- A person who was financially dependent on the deceased

The court looks at factors such as the applicant's needs, the size of the estate, other people's needs and the relationship. It does not simply re-write the will because the result seems unfair.

## Time limits

Time limits matter, and some are short. For claims under the 1975 Act, the usual period is six months from the date the grant of probate or administration is issued, although the court can sometimes allow late claims in limited circumstances. For other types of challenge, there may not be a single fixed limit, but delay can weaken a case and make it harder to prove. A solicitor can tell you which deadline applies and when it expires.

## Entering a caveat

If you are worried that a grant is about to be issued and you want to challenge the will, you can enter a caveat at the Probate Registry. It blocks a grant from being issued for a limited period and can be renewed. It does not cancel the will or guarantee you will win, and there are rules and costs around it. Take advice before relying on one.

## Who can contest a will?

Generally you need some kind of interest in the estate. That usually means someone named in a current or earlier will, someone who would inherit if there were no will, or someone who falls into one of the categories that can claim provision. If you will not benefit either way, you may not have the standing to bring a claim.

## What does it cost, and who pays?

Contested estate disputes are one of the more expensive areas of legal work. Costs depend on the complexity, the number of parties and how far the dispute goes. Courts encourage people to settle where possible, and refusing to engage in mediation or similar steps can count against you on costs.

Often, the losing side is ordered to pay a share of the winner's costs, but that is not guaranteed, and sometimes costs come out of the estate. Fee arrangements vary. Ask any solicitor for a clear estimate and for their view of the risks before you proceed.

## Alternatives to court

Many disputes settle before trial. Options include a negotiated agreement, mediation, and in some situations a family arrangement (a variation of the will with everyone's consent). These can be quicker and cheaper and keep family relationships intact.

## What to do next

1. **Don't act on your own.** Do not remove anything from the property, and don't confront family members in anger.
2. **Write down what happened** while it is fresh: dates, who said what, how the person was at the time.
3. **Find the paperwork.** Copies of wills, letters from solicitors, medical information and anything showing the person's wishes.
4. **Check the dates.** Establish when the grant was issued, if it has been.
5. **Speak to a solicitor who deals with contested wills.** Ask about their experience, their fees and how they see your position.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does it cost to contest a will?

There is no standard figure. It depends on the grounds, the evidence needed, how many people are involved and whether it settles or goes to a hearing. A solicitor should explain the likely costs and risks at the start.

### Can I contest a will if I am not mentioned in it?

Possibly, but you need to have a reason that the law recognises, such as being a person who can claim provision, or having grounds to say the will is invalid. Not being mentioned is not, by itself, enough.

### How long do I have to contest a will?

It depends on the type of claim. Some have a clear time limit, such as six months from the grant for a claim under the 1975 Act. Do not wait. Speak to a solicitor as soon as you can.

### Can I contest a will after probate has been granted?

Yes, in many cases, but things get harder once the estate has been distributed. If you think you have a claim, act quickly.

### What are the chances of success?

It depends on the evidence. Cases based on suspicion alone rarely succeed. A solicitor can give you an honest view after looking at the facts.

### Can an executor be removed?

In some circumstances the court can remove or replace an executor, for example if they are not carrying out their duties properly. That is a separate type of application from contesting the will itself.

### What if the will is a home-made will?

Home-made wills are not automatically invalid, but they can have problems with signing, witnessing and wording that lead to disputes. A solicitor can review it.

### Does a will that excludes my child count as valid?

In England and Wales, people are generally free to leave their estate to whoever they like, but certain people can apply for provision. Take advice on your own circumstances.
